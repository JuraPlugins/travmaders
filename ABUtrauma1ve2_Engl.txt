Psychology of Trauma
Course Aim
This course introduces psychological trauma and psychological, neurobiological, and behavioural responses to traumatic experiences.
We will examine individual differences in responses to traumatic experiences, including common stress reactions and clinically significant difficulties.
We will also discuss risk and protective factors, trauma-related clinical processes, and post-traumatic growth.
The course introduces psychosocial support in trauma, crisis, and disaster settings, along with relevant clinical and ethical principles.

--- Slide 11 ---
What Do We Mean by Traumatic Experiences?
Psychological trauma can follow exposure to an event involving actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence. Responses vary across people and contexts.
Exposure may involve directly experiencing or witnessing an event, learning that it happened to someone close, or repeated/extreme exposure to aversive details through one’s work.
The effects of trauma may extend across families and communities through social, developmental, and contextual pathways. Biological mechanisms remain an active area of research.
Adversity may arise from deliberate harm, accidents, neglect, or wider social conditions.
Traumatic events may result from natural hazards, accidents, interpersonal violence, or other human actions.
11
Sources: American Psychiatric Association (2022); Brewin et al. (2009); Yehuda & Lehrner (2018)

--- Slide 12 ---
During and Immediately After a Traumatic Event
Attention, memory, and familiar coping strategies may be affected during or soon after an overwhelming event.
People may experience fear, helplessness, horror, numbness, or confusion.
The event may disrupt a person’s sense of safety and predictability.
Some people describe a clear “before and after.” This is one possible experience, not a universal response.
12
Source: Ozer et al. (2003)

--- Slide 13 ---
Trauma and Adversity
DSM-5-TR Criterion A events involve actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence.
The likelihood of PTSD varies by event type and by personal, social, and contextual factors.
Other serious adversities, including chronic abuse, discrimination, and deprivation, can also have substantial effects on mental health, even when they do not meet Criterion A.
Describe these experiences precisely. “Big T” and “little t” are informal labels, not diagnostic categories.
13
Sources: Alisic et al. (2014); McLaughlin & Sheridan (2016)

--- Slide 14 ---
Collective Trauma
Collective trauma describes events or conditions that affect groups and communities and may shape shared memory, identity, and social life. The term is used across research traditions and does not refer to a single diagnosis.
War, genocide, and political oppression
Forced migration and displacement
Racism, discrimination, and gender-based violence
14
Source: Hirschberger (2018)

--- Slide 15 ---
Collective Trauma
PTSD risk differs across event types. Interpersonal violence is associated with elevated risk in many studies, but no simple rule ranks every human-caused event against every natural disaster.
15
Source: Liu et al. (2017), WHO World Mental Health Surveys

--- Slide 16 ---
Secondary traumatic stress describes trauma-related symptoms that can arise through indirect exposure, especially repeated professional exposure to traumatic details. Learning about an event through media does not automatically meet DSM-5-TR PTSD Criterion A.
Indirect exposure to others’ experiences
Repeated exposure to distressing media content
Repeated occupational exposure to traumatic details
Secondary Traumatic Stress
16
Sources: Brewin et al. (2009); Cocker & Joss (2016)

--- Slide 17 ---
Compassion Fatigue and Secondary Traumatic Stress
“Compassion fatigue” is used in different ways across the literature. It may refer to emotional exhaustion, secondary traumatic stress, or a combination of work-related strain and exposure to others’ suffering.
Emotional exhaustion or numbing
Irritability
Withdrawal or reduced engagement
Sleep problems or physical tension
17
Source: Cocker & Joss (2016)

--- Slide 18 ---
Complex Trauma and Complex PTSD
Complex trauma commonly refers to repeated or prolonged exposure to traumatic events, often in situations that are difficult to escape.
Complex PTSD is a specific diagnosis in ICD-11. It includes core PTSD symptoms and disturbances in self-organisation. Complex trauma and Complex PTSD are related terms, but they are not interchangeable.
18
Sources: WHO (2024); Karatzias et al. (2017)

--- Slide 19 ---
Childhood Adversity and Trauma
Adverse experiences during childhood or adolescence may:
involve threat, violence, loss, deprivation, or instability in caregiving and the environment;
occur once or repeatedly over time; and
affect emotional, cognitive, social, and physical development. Use “trauma” with care and attend to the child’s experience and context.
19
Source: McLaughlin & Sheridan (2016)

--- Slide 21 ---
PTSD is associated, at the group level, with differences in threat processing and brain structure or function. Findings vary across studies and do not describe every person exposed to trauma.
Findings in PTSD research
Neuroimaging research has reported:
altered amygdala responses to threat
smaller hippocampal volume in some PTSD samples
differences in prefrontal activity and regulation
alterations in stress-response systems, including the HPA axis
Neuroscience: Traumatic Stress
21
Sources: O’Doherty et al. (2015); McLaughlin et al. (2019)

--- Slide 22 ---
Individual Responses and Recovery
Traumatic experiences may involve loss, danger, or major disruption.
People may feel helpless, although reactions differ.
Some people develop persistent symptoms; others recover with time or support. Risk and protective factors operate at individual, interpersonal, and social levels.
Exposure to the same event does not lead to the same outcome for everyone.
Post-traumatic growth is reported by some people and can coexist with distress. It is a possibility, not an expected outcome.
22
Source: Wu et al. (2019)

--- Slide 23 ---
From Experience to Clinical Definition
A Clinical Definition
The word “trauma” is used broadly in everyday and clinical language. PTSD, however, has specific diagnostic criteria, including a defined exposure criterion.
23
Sources: American Psychiatric Association (2022); Brewin et al. (2009)

--- Slide 24 ---
PTSD and the DSM
Historical Background
Historical accounts of reactions to war and loss
“Shell shock” during World War I
“War neurosis” and “battle fatigue” during World War II
“Gross stress reaction” in DSM-I (1952)
“Transient situational disturbance” in DSM-II (1968)
PTSD was introduced in DSM-III in 1980
The criteria were revised in later editions; the current text revision is DSM-5-TR (2022).
24
Source: US National Center for PTSD, History of PTSD
