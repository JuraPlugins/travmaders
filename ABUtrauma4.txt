Neural Regulation of Autonomic Stress Responses
Sympathetic Nervous System
It is the “fight-or-flight” system. It is activated in situations of danger or stress. Its purpose is to mobilize energy.
Effects:
Heart rate increases
Pupils dilate
Breathing quickens
Digestion slows down
Adrenaline is released from the adrenal glands
This system prepares the body to be “ready and alert” in the face of a threat.

--- Slide 6 ---
Neural Regulation of Autonomic Stress Responses
Parasympathetic Nervous System
It is active during relaxation and rest
Effects:
Heart rate slows
Breathing becomes calmer
Digestion speeds up
Pupils constrict
Energy is conserved and the body’s systems return to balance

Neural Regulation of Autonomic Stress Responses
Brain circuits involved
Brainstem and hypothalamus:
Integrate information about the body’s internal state and help organize autonomic and endocrine responses.
Cortisol:
It is the primary stress hormone. In the short term, it facilitates energy mobilization and suppresses the immune system. In the long term, however, it can have negative effects on memory, the immune system, and mood.

Neural Regulation of Autonomic Stress Responses
Key idea
Acute stress is the process by which the brain integrates information about stressors and the body’s state across its neural networks, thereby regulating the autonomic nervous system and the HPA axis in a manner appropriate to the situation.
The two major response systems
Autonomic nervous system:
Helps regulate rapid bodily responses to a challenge.
The stress response is a coordinated brain–body process. It engages the
autonomic nervous system
and the
hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical (HPA) axis
.
HPA axis:
Produces an endocrine response through the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and adrenal cortex.
The systems are distinct but closely connected. Their relative contribution depends partly on the nature and intensity of the stressor.
Their activity is adjusted according to the type and intensity of the stressor; the response is not a fixed “fight-or-flight” pattern.

Acute Stress Reaction and Acute Stress Disorder
Chronic stress has been found to be associated with the sensitization of the HPA and autonomic responses.
Increased arousal, startle responses, changes in sleep and attention, emotional fluctuations, and ruminating on the event may be observed.
ASD, on the other hand, is a diagnostic category for post-traumatic symptoms that meet specific criteria regarding duration, number of symptoms, and the threshold for functional impairment.

--- Slide 15 ---
When is a clinical evaluation necessary?
In clinical terms, “trauma” is not merely the everyday meaning of a shocking or distressing event.
If symptoms are severe, persistent, or worsening, and are significantly impairing daily functioning, an evaluation is necessary.
Priority is given to safety risks, thoughts of self-harm, severe dissociation, substance use, or inability to meet basic needs.
For diagnosis, the nature of the event and the symptoms are examined, as well as duration, functional impairment, differential diagnosis, and context.

--- Slide 16 ---
ASB: DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Framework
Trauma criteria:
Death or threat of death, serious injury, or exposure to sexual violence; witnessing such an event; learning that it happened to a close relative; or occupational repeated exposure.
Duration of symptoms: at least 3 days and up to 1 month following the traumatic event.
At least 9 of the 14 symptoms; any combination from the domains of negative emotional states, dissociation, avoidance, and hyperarousal.
Symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or impairment; they must not be better explained by substance use, medication, or a medical condition.

History of PTSD
Herodotus recounts that the person who witnessed his friend’s death was blind.
Samuel Pepys and His Observations of the Great Fire of London in 1666
Samuel Pepys, a survivor of this great disaster that left a large portion of London’s population homeless and reduced the city to ashes, vividly described the delayed psychological effects of the trauma in his diaries.
•    Pepys wrote that even months after the fire, he struggled to sleep at night, constantly saw flames in his dreams, and would wake up in a state of great terror.
•    Unable to shake off the psychology of the fire, he would panic at the slightest whiff of smoke or crackling sound, overcome by the fear that the city was once again engulfed in flames.

--- Slide 21 ---
History of PTSD
The Industrial Revolution and the
“Railway Spine”
The impact of psychological trauma on people was observed on a much larger scale in the 19th century through train accidents and fires.
The fact that people who survived these accidents without physical injury (or with only minor scrapes) experienced intense fear, palpitations, and paralysis-like sensations for months was referred to at the time as the “Railway Spine” or Erichsen’s Disease.
This period marked the first institutionalized discussion
of the effects of trauma on the nervous system.
21

--- Slide 22 ---
History of PTSD
Wars and the Medicalization of the Concept
It was through wars that trauma came to be scientifically defined.
Shell Shock
: Observed in soldiers exposed to intense artillery fire and explosions in the trenches during
World War I.
World War II
and afterward:
war neurosis, stress reactions
, and long-term symptoms among veterans.
The 1970s:
Clinical and social movements involving Vietnam veterans and other trauma-exposed groups accelerated the process of diagnostic recognition.
In 1980, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) was officially recognized as a disorder in the DSM-III (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders).

The Importance of Defining PTSD
Early identification and timely intervention are important for reducing the risk of persistent symptoms and supporting recovery.
People with PTSD may have approximately four times the risk of a suicide attempt compared with the general population.
An estimated 41–86% of people experience at least one potentially traumatic event during their lifetime.
Lifetime PTSD prevalence has been estimated at 7–8% in U.S.A. population-based studies, and symptoms may persist for years without appropriate treatment.
Genetic, biological, and psychosocial factors are being investigated to explain why PTSD develops in some individuals but not others.
