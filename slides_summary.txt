What Do We Mean by Traumatic Experiences?
Psychological trauma can follow exposure to an event involving actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence. Responses vary across people and contexts.
Exposure may involve directly experiencing or witnessing an event, learning that it happened to someone close, or repeated/extreme exposure to aversive details through one’s work.
The effects of trauma may extend across families and communities through social, developmental, and contextual pathways. Biological mechanisms remain an active area of research.
Adversity may arise from deliberate harm, accidents, neglect, or wider social conditions.
Traumatic events may result from natural hazards, accidents, interpersonal violence, or other human actions.
11
Sources: American Psychiatric Association (2022); Brewin et al. (2009); Yehuda & Lehrner (2018)

--- Slide 12 ---
During and Immediately After a Traumatic Event
Attention, memory, and familiar coping strategies may be affected during or soon after an overwhelming event.
People may experience fear, helplessness, horror, numbness, or confusion.
The event may disrupt a person’s sense of safety and predictability.
Some people describe a clear “before and after.” This is one possible experience, not a universal response.
12
Source: Ozer et al. (2003)

Trauma and Adversity
DSM-5-TR Criterion A events involve actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence.
The likelihood of PTSD varies by event type and by personal, social, and contextual factors.
Other serious adversities, including chronic abuse, discrimination, and deprivation, can also have substantial effects on mental health, even when they do not meet Criterion A.
Describe these experiences precisely. “Big T” and “little t” are informal labels, not diagnostic categories.

Neural Regulation of Autonomic Stress Responses
Sympathetic Nervous System
It is the “fight-or-flight” system. It is activated in situations of danger or stress. Its purpose is to mobilize energy.
Effects:
Heart rate increases
Pupils dilate
Breathing quickens
Digestion slows down
Adrenaline is released from the adrenal glands
This system prepares the body to be “ready and alert” in the face of a threat.

--- Slide 6 ---
Neural Regulation of Autonomic Stress Responses
Parasympathetic Nervous System
It is active during relaxation and rest
Effects:
Heart rate slows
Breathing becomes calmer
Digestion speeds up
Pupils constrict
Energy is conserved and the body’s systems return to balance

Brainstem and hypothalamus:
Integrate information about the body’s internal state and help organize autonomic and endocrine responses.
Cortisol:

--- Slide 11 ---
Neural Regulation of Autonomic Stress Responses
Key idea
Acute stress is the process by which the brain integrates information about stressors and the body’s state across its neural networks, thereby regulating the autonomic nervous system and the HPA axis in a manner appropriate to the situation.
The two major response systems
Autonomic nervous system:
Helps regulate rapid bodily responses to a challenge.
The stress response is a coordinated brain–body process. It engages the
autonomic nervous system
and the
hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical (HPA) axis
.
HPA axis:
Produces an endocrine response through the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and adrenal cortex.
The systems are distinct but closely connected. Their relative contribution depends partly on the nature and intensity of the stressor.
Their activity is adjusted according to the type and intensity of the stressor; the response is not a fixed “fight-or-flight” pattern.

The psychological stress response is influenced by evaluation and past experience.
Limbic structures, memory, and reward circuits shape this response depending on the context.
The autonomic nervous system and the HPA response are interconnected.

--- Slide 15 ---
When is a clinical evaluation necessary?
In clinical terms, “trauma” is not merely the everyday meaning of a shocking or distressing event.
If symptoms are severe, persistent, or worsening, and are significantly impairing daily functioning, an evaluation is necessary.
Priority is given to safety risks, thoughts of self-harm, severe dissociation, substance use, or inability to meet basic needs.
For diagnosis, the nature of the event and the symptoms are examined, as well as duration, functional impairment, differential diagnosis, and context.

--- Slide 16 ---
ASB: DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Framework
Trauma criteria:
Death or threat of death, serious injury, or exposure to sexual violence; witnessing such an event; learning that it happened to a close relative; or occupational repeated exposure.
Duration of symptoms: at least 3 days and up to 1 month following the traumatic event.
At least 9 of the 14 symptoms; any combination from the domains of negative emotional states, dissociation, avoidance, and hyperarousal.
Symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or impairment; they must not be better explained by substance use, medication, or a medical condition.

--- Slide 20 ---
History of PTSD
Herodotus recounts that the person who witnessed his friend’s death was blind.
Samuel Pepys and His Observations of the Great Fire of London in 1666
Samuel Pepys, a survivor of this great disaster that left a large portion of London’s population homeless and reduced the city to ashes, vividly described the delayed psychological effects of the trauma in his diaries.
•    Pepys wrote that even months after the fire, he struggled to sleep at night, constantly saw flames in his dreams, and would wake up in a state of great terror.
•    Unable to shake off the psychology of the fire, he would panic at the slightest whiff of smoke or crackling sound, overcome by the fear that the city was once again engulfed in flames.
20

--- Slide 21 ---
History of PTSD
The Industrial Revolution and the
“Railway Spine”
The impact of psychological trauma on people was observed on a much larger scale in the 19th century through train accidents and fires.
The fact that people who survived these accidents without physical injury (or with only minor scrapes) experienced intense fear, palpitations, and paralysis-like sensations for months was referred to at the time as the “Railway Spine” or Erichsen’s Disease.
This period marked the first institutionalized discussion
of the effects of trauma on the nervous system.
21

--- Slide 22 ---
History of PTSD
Wars and the Medicalization of the Concept
It was through wars that trauma came to be scientifically defined.
Shell Shock
: Observed in soldiers exposed to intense artillery fire and explosions in the trenches during
World War I.
World War II
and afterward:
war neurosis, stress reactions
, and long-term symptoms among veterans.
The 1970s:
Clinical and social movements involving Vietnam veterans and other trauma-exposed groups accelerated the process of diagnostic recognition.
In 1980, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) was officially recognized as a disorder in the DSM-III (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders).
22

--- Slide 23 ---
History of PTSD
The DSM-5-TR retains the four symptom clusters and the broad range of symptoms; dissociative symptoms are defined as a separate feature.
The ICD-11 defines PTSD with a narrower set of core symptoms: re-experiencing, avoidance, and a persistent sense of threat.
The ICD-11 also defines complex PTSD as PTSD with additional symptoms of difficulty regulating emotions, a negative self-image, and relationship difficulties.
23

--- Slide 24 ---
Key changes to the PTSD criteria from DSM-IV to DSM-5
Indirect exposure was specified to include learning that a traumatic event occurred to a
close family member or close friend
.
In cases involving death or threatened death, it must have been violent or accidental.
Criterion A was also expanded to include
repeated or extreme exposure to aversive details
of traumatic events, typically in the course of professional duties.
This does not include exposure through electronic media, television unless it is work-related.

The Importance of Defining PTSD
Early identification and timely intervention are important for reducing the risk of persistent symptoms and supporting recovery.
People with PTSD may have approximately four times the risk of a suicide attempt compared with the general population.
An estimated 41–86% of people experience at least one potentially traumatic event during their lifetime.
Lifetime PTSD prevalence has been estimated at 7–8% in U.S.A. population-based studies, and symptoms may persist for years without appropriate treatment.
Genetic, biological, and psychosocial factors are being investigated to explain why PTSD develops in some individuals but not others.

PTSD Criterion Sets in the DSM-5-TR
Criterion B: Intrusion Symptoms
The presence of one or more of the following symptoms associated with the traumatic event, beginning after the event:
Recurrent, involuntary, and intrusive distressing memories of the traumatic event.
Recurrent distressing dreams in which the content and/or affect of the dream are related to the traumatic event.
Dissociative reactions (e.g., flashbacks) in which the individual feels or acts as if the traumatic event were recurring.
Intense or prolonged psychological distress at exposure to internal or external cues that symbolize or resemble an aspect of the traumatic event.
Marked physiological reactions to internal or external cues that symbolize or resemble an aspect of the traumatic event.

PTSD Criterion Sets in the DSM-5-TR
Criterion C: Avoidance
Persistent avoidance of stimuli associated with the traumatic event, beginning after the event occurred, as evidenced by one or both of the following:
Avoidance of, or efforts to avoid, distressing memories, thoughts, or feelings about or closely associated with the traumatic event.
Avoidance of, or efforts to avoid, external reminders (e.g., people, places, conversations, activities, objects, or situations) that arouse distressing memories, thoughts, or feelings about or closely associated with the traumatic event.

--- Slide 6 ---
PTSD Criterion Sets in the DSM-5-TR
Criterion D: Negative Alterations in Cognitions and Mood
Two or more of the following
Inability to remember an important aspect of the traumatic event.
Persistent and exaggerated negative beliefs or expectations about oneself, others, or the world.
Persistent, distorted cognitions about the cause or consequences of the traumatic event that lead the individual to blame themselves or others.
A persistent negative emotional state.
Markedly diminished interest in or participation in significant activities.
Feelings of detachment or estrangement from others.
Persistent difficulty experiencing positive emotions.

Criterion E: Alterations in Arousal and Reactivity
Two or more of the following:
Irritable behavior and angry outbursts, typically expressed as verbal or physical aggression toward people or objects, with little or no provocation.
Reckless or self-destructive behavior.
Hypervigilance.

Problems with concentration.

The disturbance is not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance or another medical condition.
